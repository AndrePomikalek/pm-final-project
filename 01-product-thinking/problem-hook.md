## Cold-read your own hook
- **Is the business risk high enough to justify a new initiative, a high-stakes threat, or a minor inconvenience?:** The business risk justifies taking the initiative. It poses a serious threat to the installment process, the self-service strategy, and the portfolio value.

The briefing does not describe a failure of the entire company. It describes recurring damage occurring precisely where Riverty intends to conclude transactions digitally.

Each month, 100,000 consumers receive a relevant message. 38% open the portal, but only 22% of them start an installment process, and only 46% of those who start it complete the transaction. Of those who start the process, 31% call within seven days. Afterward, 28% of the proof-of-income documents still require clarification. 19% of new contracts default within 60 days. More than half of the administrative workload is tied up in payments, legal matters, and enforcement.

The risk lies in three layers:

Client. Responsible debt collection, reputation, transparent reporting, and contractual and regulatory obligations all depend on whether plans are understood and adhered to.
Our own portfolios. Riverty bears the financial risk itself. Commitments that look good in the short term but fail later on diminish the long-term portfolio value.
Management. The self-service rate counts a plan as resolved as soon as it’s created. The metric used by management to steer the business may rise, while outcomes for consumers, clients, and portfolios deteriorate.
One inconvenience would be a cumbersome click in an otherwise smooth process. This is where the digital onboarding breaks down, creating avoidable work for employees and counting unstable plans as successes. That’s why we’re launching Spotlight now: to provide guidance before commitment, so that the next self-service rate counts plans that are actually adhered to.
- **Is the moment of misery a systemic problem or just an edge case?:** This is a systemic problem. Mara represents a recurring pattern in the billing process, not an isolated case.

The briefing describes the process itself as fragmented: the portal, letters, digital reminders, decision-making logic, and employee workflows operate in isolation from one another. Early research identifies four recurring themes, not isolated cases: consumers cannot identify a feasible repayment plan, do not understand the income questions, fear providing incorrect information or missing a payment, and employees receive insufficient context when taking over a case.

The figures apply to the entire population, not to an exception. Of the 100,000 consumers contacted each month, 38% open the portal; only 22% of them initiate an installment plan; 31% of those who start the process call within seven days; 28% of the supporting documents require further clarification; and 19% of new contracts default within 60 days. More than half of the case worker’s workload is tied up in payment, legal matters, and enforcement.

Mara’s two outstanding claims are also explicitly covered in the pilot program, alongside cases involving only one claim. Vulnerable consumers and uncertain exceptions require a separate escalation process. The moment when someone on the phone isn’t sure whether the plan is feasible and therefore hangs up, calls back, or agrees incorrectly is the typical scenario for anyone who cannot pay in full at once.
- **Does the value proposition actually remove the obstacle you identified, or is it a generic feature?:** The value proposition removes the identified obstacle. It is tied to Mara’s specific questions before she commits, not to a general feature of the portal.

The obstacle is: She has little time and cannot tell whether the payment plan is affordable, why she is being asked about her income, what happens if she misses a payment, and whether the two requirements go hand in hand. If these questions remain unanswered, she’ll abandon the process, call, or agree to a plan she can’t stick to.

Spotlight addresses exactly these four points before she agrees:

Which payment amount fits her fluctuating income.
Why the income information is necessary.
What happens if she misses a payment.
Whether both debts can be combined into a single repayment plan.
Daniel receives the input, the rationale, and the handoff. This eliminates the need to repeat the review process, which currently arises from this same obstacle.

A generic solution might involve a chat, a shorter process, or more closed deals—without addressing these questions. That would boost the self-service rate but leave the obstacle in place.

One gap remains: proof of income, which she cannot find on her cell phone. 28% of uploads still require clarification afterward. Spotlight resolves the uncertainty before approval. It only eliminates the requirement for documentation if the plan is finalized with a verifiable statement and proof is requested only in uncertain cases.
